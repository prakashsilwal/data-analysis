I.

Abstract

This study explored and anticipated US youth tobacco consumption patterns using advanced statistical and machine learning techniques. Historical data was analyzed using Linear Regression and ARIMA models to predict future trends. Between 2018 and 2020, youth consumed less tobacco according to Linear Regression projections. Empirical data showed higher consumption rates, suggesting that external factors — including new tobacco products, changing social norms, and the COVID-19 pandemic — may have caused discrepancies. Clustering analysis revealed distinct state groupings with varying tobacco use levels, highlighting significant geographical variation in young people’s tobacco use across the United States.

II.

Background

Most people start smoking before they turn 18. Adding flavors to tobacco products makes them more appealing to teens and young adults. Survey data indicates that 80.2% of high school students and 74.6% of middle school students reported using flavored tobacco products in the last 30 days. Among students who used e-cigarettes in 2023, 90.3% of high school and 87.1% of middle school students reported using flavored e-cigarettes.

Since 2014, e-cigarettes have gradually become the most popular form of tobacco among young people. By 2023, approximately 1 in 22 middle school students reported using electronic cigarettes in the past 30 days. Multiple tobacco product categories were in use, including cigarettes, cigars, oral tobacco, hookahs, heated tobacco products, and nicotine pouches. About 2.5% of middle school students and 3.9% of high school students reported using more than two tobacco products in the preceding 30 days.

Youth tobacco use is influenced by a wide range of factors including social and physical environment, biology and genetics, mental health, and personal attitudes. Risk factors include low income, lack of parental support, easy product availability, poor academic performance, low self-esteem, and exposure to tobacco marketing. National, state, and community programs addressing taxation, smoke-free policies, minimum age laws, and media campaigns have all been shown to reduce youth tobacco consumption rates.

III.

Introduction

The Data Science Lifecycle is a comprehensive framework encompassing methodical guidelines, strategies, and approaches that facilitate the application of machine learning and analytical techniques to extract insights and predictions from data. This study applies that full lifecycle — from data preparation through modeling and visualization — to examine the prevalence of tobacco use among young individuals in the United States.

The dataset was collected by the Department of Health and Human Services and obtained from data.gov. It contains survey data on the number of young Americans using tobacco products, tracking trends by grade level across multiple years. Key variables include year, geographic location, gender, race, education level, and proportion of tobacco use.

A primary objective is to classify US states by their percentage of youth tobacco consumption. Through K-means clustering, this study identifies which states have high versus low rates of youth tobacco use, enabling targeted public health interventions and regional policy design. Following clustering, time series forecasting using ARIMA and Linear Regression provides projections of tobacco use trends and allows determination of the most reliable prediction method aligned with historical patterns.

The analytical framework follows the standard data science lifecycle: data understanding, cleaning, exploratory data analysis (EDA), feature engineering, model building, and result visualization — providing a systematic pathway to accurate predictions and actionable insights on youth tobacco use.

IV.

Literature Review

Case Study 1 — Tobacco Outlet Density (Rodriguez et al., 2013)

Rodriguez et al. conducted a census tract-level cross-sectional study examining sociodemographic factors and tobacco outlet density (TOD) across all 64,909 census tracts of the continental USA. Results indicated that TOD was significantly higher in urban areas and strongly associated with greater proportions of African American and Hispanic residents, and women with lower education levels. The effect for Hispanic populations was ten times larger in urban versus rural tracts — indicating that tobacco retail access is disproportionately concentrated in communities most vulnerable to adverse health outcomes.

Case Study 2 — Machine Learning for Smoking Cessation (Lai et al., 2021)

Lai et al. developed prediction models using machine learning to forecast smoking cessation outcomes for 4,875 patients enrolled in a cessation program in Northern Taiwan. Seven algorithms were tested including ANN, SVM, Random Forest, and Logistic Regression. The ANN model achieved the best performance with accuracy of 0.640 and ROC of 0.660 (95% CI: 0.617–0.702), with binary abstinence status at six months as the primary outcome.

Dataset Features

The CDC Youth Tobacco Survey dataset contains key variables: YEAR (1999–2017), LocationAbbr/LocationDesc (state identifiers), TopicType (Tobacco Use – Survey Data), TopicDesc (Cigarette Use / Smokeless Tobacco Use), MeasureDesc (Smoking Status, User Status), DataSource (YTS), Response (Ever, Frequent), Data_Value (percentage of tobacco use), GeoLocation (geographic coordinates), and demographic fields: Gender, Race, Age, Education.

V.

Pre-Processing and Cleaning

The dataset was imported into Jupyter Notebook using Python’s Pandas and NumPy libraries. Initial inspection documented total rows, columns, data types, and summary statistics. Duplicate entries were identified and removed. Two features with insufficient data were eliminated entirely. Missing values were addressed through targeted imputation: 520 missing “Response” column entries were filled with “Unknown,” while numerical missing values were replaced with column means — a standard practice for preserving dataset size without introducing directional bias. Thorough statistical examination including mean, median, mode, and quartile calculations was then performed across all numerical features.

VI.

Exploratory Data Analysis

A. Univariate Analysis

Univariate analysis focused on the “Data Value” column representing tobacco use rates. Box plots and histograms revealed a right-skewed distribution with a few outliers, indicating that most youth used tobacco at modest rates while a small proportion used it at considerably higher rates. The median tobacco use rate was approximately 20%, with an overall range spanning 0–80%.

B. Bivariate Analysis

Annual tobacco use averages were calculated by aggregating data by year. A clear and consistent declining trend was identified from 1999 to 2017, with average youth tobacco use falling from 26.60% to 12.66% — a reduction of more than half over 18 years.

VII.

Feature Engineering

Feature engineering centered on calculating the mean tobacco usage per state, enabling classification of states with comparable consumption rates. Linear Regression and ARIMA models were applied for time series forecasting targeting mean annual tobacco consumption, facilitating understanding of longitudinal patterns and enabling future projections for policy evaluation.

VIII.

Methodology

A. K-Means Clustering

K-means partitions observations into k clusters by minimizing the sum of squared distances between each data point and its cluster centroid:

J = Σj=1k Σi=1n ‖xi(j) − Cj‖²

States were clustered by mean tobacco consumption. The elbow method identified k=3 as optimal. Each state was iteratively assigned to its nearest centroid until convergence, producing three geographically distinct groupings of tobacco use intensity.

B. Linear Regression

Linear regression modeled the relationship between tobacco use rate (dependent) and year (independent) using the least-squares best-fit line:

y = mx + c

Where y = estimated tobacco use %, m = slope (annual rate of change), x = year, c = y-intercept. The model was trained on data through 2017 and used to forecast 2018, 2019, and 2020.

C. ARIMA Model

ARIMA (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average) captures patterns in historical time series to project future values. Defined by parameters p (autoregressive order), d (differencing degree), and q (moving average order):

(1 − ΣφiLi)(1 − L)d Yt = (1 + ΣθiLi)εt

The time series was first-order differenced for stationarity. ACF and PACF plots informed parameter selection. Final parameters used:

X.

Comparison of Models and Implications

Linear Regression clearly outperformed ARIMA. With R²=0.73 and low error metrics, it captured the consistent downward trajectory and produced declining forecasts (12.55% → 11.86% → 11.16%) aligned with historical patterns. ARIMA produced a negative R²=−0.57, higher errors, and near-flat projections (~12.8% across all three years), failing to leverage the strong directional signal in the data — likely due to the limited 18-point time series being insufficient for ARIMA to learn complex temporal dependencies.

Linear Regression is recommended for this analysis due to its interpretability, alignment with historical trends, and superior predictive accuracy — making it a reliable tool for informing public health strategies targeting youth tobacco use reduction.

XI.

Discussion

Linear Regression forecast rates of 12.55% (2018), 11.86% (2019), and 11.16% (2020). However, observed data showed higher actual rates — approximately 17.15% in 2018, 21.85% in 2019, and 12.19% in 2020. These discrepancies reveal important contextual factors:

Model Linearity Assumptions: Real-world tobacco use may follow non-linear trajectories during periods of social disruption or product innovation, causing linear model predictions to deviate from observed values.

External Influences: Legislative changes, public health campaigns, and the rapid emergence of e-cigarettes and vaping devices significantly impacted use rates in ways not captured by the historical trend line.

Social and Cultural Dynamics: The notable spike in 2019 likely reflects the dramatic rise in youth vaping. Social media exposure, peer influence, and aggressive marketing of flavored e-cigarettes created a new wave of nicotine initiation not predictable from traditional tobacco trend data.

COVID-19 Pandemic: The pandemic disrupted tobacco use patterns in 2020 through changed social environments, increased stress, altered purchasing behavior, and shifts in survey methodology and sampling.

These findings underscore the complexity of behavioral forecasting and highlight the need for models that incorporate external variables, non-linear dynamics, and real-time contextual data.

XII.

Conclusion

This study applied predictive clustering and time series analysis to examine and forecast patterns of tobacco use among youth in the United States. Linear Regression successfully modeled the historical declining trend (R²=0.73), while ARIMA proved less effective for this short, consistently trending time series. K-means clustering revealed three distinct geographic groupings with meaningful differences in average tobacco consumption rates, with Cluster 0 states averaging more than double the rates of Cluster 1 states.

The gap between predicted and actual values for 2018–2020 highlights the need for more sophisticated models capable of incorporating non-linear dynamics, external contextual variables, and demographic shifts. Future work should explore ensemble approaches, neural network models, and integration of real-time behavioral data to improve prediction accuracy.

This research contributes evidence-based insights into the trajectory of youth tobacco use and demonstrates the value of data science methodologies applied to critical public health challenges. The findings support targeted regional policy interventions — particularly in high-use Cluster 0 states — aimed at accelerating tobacco use reduction among young people nationwide.
